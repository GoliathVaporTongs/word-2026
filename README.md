## What people usually mean by "word"

When someone types a bare `word` into a search box, they almost never mean the English noun. They mean one of four things, roughly in this order of likelihood:

1. **Microsoft Word** — the application. They want to open it, use it, or get it.
2. **A `.docx` file** — they have a document and need something that can open it.
3. **A free alternative** — they don't want to pay, and they want to know what actually works.
4. **Word online** — they want the browser version, not the installed one.

The `word+` phrasing sits awkwardly across all four, which is exactly why search engines throw a mixed bag at anyone who types it. If you landed here from that query, pick the section below that matches your real intent and skip the rest.

## The browser version is the one most people actually want

Microsoft Word has a web edition that runs at `word.cloud.microsoft` (and previously at `office.com`, which now redirects). It is genuinely free for personal use with a Microsoft account, and it opens `.docx` files without complaint. This is the answer to `ms word online free` and `microsoft word online free` in nearly every case.

What you get in the browser:

- Full editing of `.docx` documents, including comments and track changes.
- Real-time co-editing — two people in the same document, cursors visible.
- Autosave to OneDrive, plus the option to download a `.docx` copy.
- A reduced ribbon. Layout tools, mail merge, and some field codes are missing or limited.

What you don't get: the desktop-only features. If you rely on macros (VBA), complex cross-references, custom stylesheets applied across a long manuscript, or the full References tab, the browser will frustrate you. For a cover letter, a school essay, or a shared meeting note, it's more than enough.

## Free alternatives that open `.docx` properly

If the point of your search was `free word processor` rather than Microsoft's own product, here's the honest shortlist. All of these read and write `.docx`, and all of them have a real desktop build.

- **LibreOffice Writer** — the closest thing to a drop-in replacement. Handles `.docx`, `.odt`, `.rtf`, and `.doc`. The interface feels dated, and very complex Word documents can shift layout slightly on import, but for everyday documents it's solid and completely free.
- **OnlyOffice Desktop Editors** — a more Word-like ribbon than LibreOffice, with strong `.docx` fidelity because it shares rendering code with its online suite. Good choice if you collaborate with people who live in Word.
- **Google Docs** — not a desktop app, but it opens `.docx` from Drive and exports back cleanly. The trade-off is that Google's format conversion can nudge spacing and fonts.
- **WordPad** — the one built into older Windows installs. It opens `.docx` in recent versions but strips most formatting. Treat it as a viewer, not an editor. Microsoft has been removing it from newer Windows builds, so don't build a workflow on it.

A quick gotcha that trips people up constantly: `.doc` and `.docx` are different formats. `.doc` is the old binary format; `.docx` is the zipped XML format introduced in 2007. Every modern tool opens both, but anything older than about 2003 may only understand `.doc`.

## Getting Word without guessing at download pages

`ms word download` and `microsoft word download` are queries with a lot of noise around them. The clean answer:

- **Microsoft 365** is the subscription that includes the desktop Word app. It installs via the Microsoft Store or the official Microsoft download page.
- **Office on the web** needs no download at all — it's the browser version covered above.
- **Mobile** — the Word app for iOS and Android is free for basic viewing and editing on phones and small tablets.

The important habit: get the installer from Microsoft's own site or your platform's app store, and nowhere else. Search results for these queries are crowded with pages that look official and aren't.

## Why "word+" specifically

The trailing plus is a leftover from search syntax that most engines no longer honour the way they once did. Google dropped the `+` operator years ago and now treats it as punctuation. Bing and DuckDuckGo handle it inconsistently. So `word+` ends up behaving almost exactly like `word`, with a slight nudge toward results that contain the literal token.

That's the whole story of this query: it's a search for *word*, with a piece of dead syntax attached. If you got here, the useful thing isn't the plus — it's figuring out which of the four meanings above you actually wanted. Most people wanted the browser version. Some wanted a free desktop replacement. A few just had a `.docx` file and no idea what to open it with.

Pick your lane, and you're done in a minute.
